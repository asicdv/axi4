The 4KB Boundary Rule

Case 10 of 10 · Why a burst may not cross a page, and how masters must split
—

0 Background read this first — context before mechanics

A single INCR burst must not cross a 4KB-aligned address boundary. It is the one absolute address rule in AXI4, and the reason is nothing to do with the bus — it is about what a page means.

Why 4KB

4KB is the smallest page size in essentially every MMU that matters. A page is the unit at which the system assigns properties, and two adjacent pages can differ in every one of them:

PropertyWhy it breaks a straddling burst
Physical mappingVirtually contiguous pages are frequently not physically contiguous. The second half of the burst belongs at a completely different physical address
Memory typeOne page normal, the next device. Half the burst may be merged and buffered; half must not be
PermissionsOne page writable, the next read-only. Half the burst is permitted and half is a violation
Target slaveThe decoder may route the two halves to entirely different slaves

A burst carries one address and one set of attributes. If it spans two pages, there is no correct interpretation — which is why the protocol forbids it outright rather than defining behaviour.

The check

end_addr = AWADDR + (AWLEN+1) × 2^AWSIZE − 1
legal ⟺ AWADDR[31:12] == end_addr[31:12]

// 0x0FF0 + 32 − 1 = 0x100F
// page 0x0 vs page 0x1 → ILLEGAL

Which burst types are affected

Only INCR can violate it

FIXED never leaves one address. WRAP is confined to its own aligned block of at most 2KB. Only INCR walks forward far enough to cross a boundary, so only INCR needs the check.

Splitting

A master that needs to transfer across a boundary issues two bursts: one ending at the last address in the first page, one starting at the base of the next. In this case a 32-byte transfer from 0x0FF0 becomes 16 bytes from 0x0FF0 and 16 bytes from 0x1000, with two AW handshakes and two responses.

DMA engines do this automatically and it is one of the things worth checking in a new one. Hand-written masters and simple test benches frequently do not, which is why this is a recurring bring-up bug.

The failure mode

What happens when you violate it is implementation-defined, and that is the problem. A well-behaved interconnect returns DECERR. A less careful one may route the whole burst to the slave that owns the first address — so the tail of the transfer lands at the wrong physical location, in a different device's registers, with no error at all.

Silent misdirection into another peripheral's address space is the worst realistic outcome, and it is why this check belongs in your protocol monitor rather than only in the master.
